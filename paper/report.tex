\tnotetext[wr]{Draft, prepared with Claude Code. Results are on the validation period; the test period has not been used.}

\author[ytu]{H. Oğuz Bolat\corref{cor}}
\ead{oguz.bolat@std.yildiz.edu.tr}
\cortext[cor]{Corresponding author}
\affiliation[ytu]{organization={Department of Geomatics Engineering, Yıldız Technical University},
  city={Istanbul}, country={T\"urkiye}}

\begin{abstract}
Continuous GNSS networks are dense in a few regions and sparse in most others, yet many applications need displacement time series at locations without a station. We generate daily residual series at arbitrary locations from the stations around them with a conditional diffusion model, and judge the generated series as samples rather than as point predictions. Because a station's series is only partly predictable from its neighbours, a regression model returns series that are too smooth; a generative model can reproduce the station-specific noise while inheriting the shared signal. The model is trained and validated on 1,251 stations in and around California, where real stations can be hidden and their generated counterparts compared with the truth, and tested without retraining on T\"urkiye, whose median nearest-neighbour distance (60~km) is about six times California's. Training samples are drawn from networks thinned by minimum-separation thinning, which reproduces T\"urkiye's station spacing, and carry simple synthetic transients so that the model learns to pass on localised signals. On held-out stations the diffusion model is the most accurate probabilistic generator at every density, 3--5\% better in continuous ranked probability score than a space--time Gaussian process fitted to the same years, and its samples are the only ones that a classifier cannot reliably tell from real stations. No generator can tell from the neighbours how noisy the station at a location is; given that noise level as an optional input, the diffusion model is calibrated across stations of every noise level. Planted fault-slip events reach a virtual station only when they are larger than the gaps between stations; the model recovers a median of 41\% of the planted signal on the full network (Gaussian process 37\%), and it reproduces the offsets of the 2019 Ridgecrest earthquakes from a network thinned to 60~km with a median error of 3.7~mm, where every method trained without transients errs by 11~mm or more. Applied without retraining to T\"urkiye, it remains the best method: 1.8\% better than the Gaussian process, at 138 of 150 stations, while the national network records densely, and on par with it on the sparser network of later years.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Daily position time series from continuous GNSS stations record interseismic loading, coseismic offsets, postseismic relaxation, slow slip and environmental loading. The Nevada Geodetic Laboratory (NGL) processes more than 17,000 stations worldwide with a uniform strategy \citep{blewitt2018,ngl2026}, but their distribution is very uneven: in California the median distance between a station and its nearest neighbour is 9~km, whereas in T\"urkiye, one of the most seismically active countries in the world, it is 60~km. Deformation with a footprint smaller than the station spacing, such as shallow slow slip or afterslip on a single fault segment, is then recorded by a few stations at most, or not at all.

This paper asks whether the series that a station \emph{would} record at a location without one can be generated from the stations around it. Spatial interpolation of displacement is classical, and graph neural networks have been used to estimate time series at unobserved locations of sensor networks \citep{wu2021ignnk} and to impute missing observations \citep{cini2022grin,marisca2022spin}. These methods return a single prediction. For GNSS series this is a limitation in principle rather than in degree: a station's daily residual is the sum of a part shared with its neighbours, namely common-mode error from orbits, clocks and the reference frame, environmental loading and tectonic transients \citep{wdowinski1997,dong2006,kreemer2021,li2025loading}, and a part that is its own, namely monument instability, multipath and other site effects, whose temporally correlated noise is well documented \citep{williams2004,langbein2008}. The second part cannot be predicted from other stations. Predicting a station from its neighbours is also the basis of recent network filtering, where the prediction is subtracted as noise \citep{bachelot2025}; there, the unpredictable remainder is the desired output. Here it is the part to be reproduced.

In the benchmark that preceded this work, the best deterministic predictor from neighbours reduced the median normalised error of a held-out station's short-period variations only from 0.73, for predicting zero, to about 0.52. A regression model would therefore generate virtual stations that are systematically too smooth and too quiet, with too little variance and the wrong spectrum. A conditional generative model can instead draw the station-specific part from a learned distribution while taking the shared part from the neighbours. We use a conditional diffusion model \citep{ho2020ddpm,karras2022edm}, whose samples can be scored against the truth with proper scoring rules \citep{gneiting2007} and whose realism can be checked against the spectra and spatial correlations of real stations.

The study is designed around network densification. The model is trained and validated in California, where 173 stations are held out of training entirely and their generated counterparts compared with their real series, and it is tested without any tuning in T\"urkiye. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data, Section~\ref{sec:method} the problem, the sampling design, the model and the baselines, and Section~\ref{sec:eval} the evaluation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses them and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Shared and station-specific signal in GNSS series}

A station's daily residuals combine signal shared with its region and signal of its own. Spatial filtering, introduced for the first continuously operating array in southern California, estimates the shared part, the common-mode error, by stacking the residuals of a network and removes it from every station \citep{wdowinski1997}. Principal component analysis of the same network later showed that common-mode errors dominate daily solutions and are close to spatially uniform across southern California, which implies a source of very long wavelength \citep{dong2006}. Over larger areas the common mode varies: in western Europe, a filter built from the stations closest to each target removes more noise than a regional one, particularly in the vertical \citep{kreemer2021}. Part of the shared signal is physical, since atmospheric, hydrological and non-tidal ocean loading produce much of the nonlinear variation of coordinate time series \citep{li2025loading}. The station-specific part is correlated in time. Maximum-likelihood analyses describe it as white plus flicker noise in most solutions \citep{williams2004}, with random-walk, power-law and first-order Gauss--Markov components at many stations in southern California and Nevada \citep{langbein2008}; both studies find its amplitude to depend on the monument. \citet{benoist2020} fitted an exponential spatiotemporal covariance model to the stochastic variations of the IGS repro2 series and showed that accounting for spatial and temporal correlation together improves coordinates and velocities estimated from short series. These studies characterise the residual field that a virtual station must reproduce; they do not generate it where there is no station.

\subsection{Estimating fields and series away from stations}

Geodetic interpolation has mostly concerned static fields. Strain rates are interpolated from GNSS velocities with spatially varying smoothing and formal uncertainties \citep{shen2015}, and horizontal velocity fields with the Green's functions of a thin elastic sheet, which couple the two components \citep{sandwell2016}; both interpolate a field that does not change from day to day. For time series, \citet{tian2016} estimated the regional common mode with neighbours weighted by their distance and by the correlation of their long-term residuals with the target's, which requires a record at the target, and \citet{qiu2024} filled gaps in GNSS position series with an improved Gaussian process applied to the station's own record. Closest to the present task, \citet{li2023interp} predicted the series of each of five stations in North China from those of the others with gradient-boosted trees and random forests; for scattered missing days, the vertical precision improved by 45\% over cubic splines. These predictions are deterministic, and the target is an existing station with gaps. Gaussian-process regression \citep{rasmussen2006} and covariance functions for space--time data \citep{gneiting2002} provide the probabilistic counterpart, on which the kriging and Gaussian-process baselines of this study are built (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\subsection{Machine learning on GNSS position series}

Deep learning on GNSS position series has so far served mostly detection and denoising. \citet{costantino2023} trained a multi-station detector of slow slip events on synthetic series made of realistic noise and modelled slip, and applied it to raw series in Cascadia from 2007 to 2022, where it retrieved 87.5\% of previously catalogued events. Graph neural networks denoise the series of irregular networks whose stations come and go: trained on the same kind of synthetic data, SSEdenoiser extracts slow slip from raw Cascadia series \citep{costantino2024}, and \citet{bachelot2025} reconstructed each station's noise from its eight nearest stations beyond 40~km, reducing the common-mode noise of the horizontal components by more than 70\% and 30\% in two processing solutions. In denoising, the part of a station that its neighbours predict is removed as noise and the remainder is kept as signal; here, that part is the starting point, and the remainder is what has to be generated. A foundation model pretrained on the series of more than 17,000 stations supports forecasting and the localisation of earthquake steps, but processes each station's window on its own \citep{teutschmann2026gnssfm}. Training on synthetic transients is thus established for detection and denoising; this study uses it to teach a generator to pass signal on (Section~\ref{sec:model}) and tests the transfer on fault-slip events and on a real earthquake.

\subsection{Kriging, imputation and diffusion models}

Machine learning has addressed the same task for other sensor networks. Inductive kriging estimates series at unobserved nodes of a sensor graph with graph neural networks trained by masking observed nodes \citep{wu2021ignnk}, and \citet{xu2025kits} reduced the gap between the sparse graph seen in training and the denser one met in inference by adding virtual nodes to the training graph. Graph neural networks also impute missing values in sensor networks \citep{cini2022grin}, including very sparse observations \citep{marisca2022spin}. These predictions are deterministic. Diffusion models generate samples by reversing a gradual noising process \citep{ho2020ddpm,karras2022edm}, and conditioning, optionally with guidance \citep{ho2022cfg}, turns them into predictors. CSDI conditions a score-based diffusion model on the observed values of a multivariate time series for probabilistic imputation and evaluates it with the CRPS \citep{tashiro2021csdi}; PriSTI adds conditional features built from spatiotemporal dependencies and geographic relationships, for spatiotemporal imputation including sensor failure \citep{liu2023pristi}; and USTD treats forecasting and kriging as conditional generation, with a deterministic encoder and diffusion decoders, on traffic and air-quality networks of 36 to 358 sensors \citep{hu2024ustd}. The model of this study belongs to this family. It differs in what geodesy requires: generation at arbitrary coordinates rather than at the nodes of a fixed graph, sparse and irregular network geometries learned by thinning a dense network, evaluation of realism (spectra, inter-station correlation, calibration across stations of different noise levels) and of the transfer of physical transients, and transfer to another region without retraining.

\subsection{Judging generated series}

A scoring rule is proper if a forecaster obtains the best expected score by reporting its true predictive distribution \citep{gneiting2007}; the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) and its multivariate generalisation, the energy score, are proper scores for real-valued outcomes. For a finite ensemble, the usual CRPS favours ensembles drawn from too narrow a distribution, and a fair version corrects this \citep{ferro2014}. The coverage of an ensemble's intervals depends on how sample quantiles are defined \citep{hyndman1996}. Classifier two-sample tests ask whether a classifier trained to separate real from generated samples does better than chance \citep{lopezpaz2017}.

\subsection{This study}

To our knowledge, no previous study generates GNSS position series probabilistically at locations without a station from the surrounding network. Probabilistic kriging with diffusion models exists for traffic and air-quality sensors \citep{hu2024ustd}; GNSS series have been predicted from neighbours deterministically \citep{li2023interp} or for denoising \citep{bachelot2025}; and synthetic transients have trained detectors and denoisers \citep{costantino2023,costantino2024}. This study combines these elements for virtual GNSS stations and adds what they do not test: calibration across stations of different noise levels, the transfer of fault-slip events as a function of network spacing, a real earthquake, and transfer between regions.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{California}

We use the daily IGS20 solutions of NGL for 1,251 stations in and around California, from 2008-01-01 to 2026-09-26 (6,844 days), processed as in the preceding benchmark; rebuilt with this study's code from the same files, the data are identical to the benchmark's. For each station and component, a constant, a rate, annual and semi-annual terms and steps are fitted to the years 2008--2019; the residuals of this fit are the data. Equipment steps are estimated and removed; earthquake offsets are kept, since they are spatially coherent signal that a virtual station should reproduce. Outliers more than five robust standard deviations from a 61-day running median are removed, as are stations with excessive scatter (groundwater subsidence, unstable monuments) and the less complete member of any pair of stations closer than 1~km.

The years 2008--2019 are used for training, 2020--2021 for validation and 2022 onward for testing. Of the stations, 173 are held out (Figure~\ref{fig:network}). Held-out stations are excluded from training entirely: they are never a training target, never a neighbour, and never part of the input network when another station is the target. They serve only as evaluation targets. Days around the 2019 Ridgecrest earthquakes are excluded from training.

\subsection{T\"urkiye}

NGL provides 233 stations with at least a year of data in the box 35.8--42.2$^\circ$N, 25.6--44.9$^\circ$E, which contains T\"urkiye and parts of its neighbours: the eastern Aegean islands, Bulgaria, Georgia, Armenia, northern Iraq and Iran. They are processed with the same steps as California's, except for the fit window. Most of the series come from T\"urkiye's national continuous network, which NGL holds densely only for 2009--2015: about 140 stations record on a typical day of those years and about 17 from 2016 on (Table~\ref{tab:turkiye}). Fitting the trajectory to 2019, as in California, would leave only the sparse remainder outside the fit. The trajectory is therefore fitted to the days up to 2012, and the main scores use 2013--2015, outside the fit and with the dense network. Of the 233 stations, 207 are kept (3 have too little data for a fit, 5 fail the scatter limits and 18 are the less complete member of a pair closer than 1~km), 176 of them in T\"urkiye according to the 1:10m country polygons of \citet{naturalearth}, with two islets in the Sea of Marmara that the polygons omit assigned to the nearest country. Every station is a target, and scores are also reported for those in T\"urkiye. The T\"urkiye data are used only for testing.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem}

Let $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 3}$ be the residual series of a target location over a window of $L = 256$ days, in east, north and up. The conditioning information $\mathcal{C}$ consists of the $K = 16$ nearest stations of a network within 300~km of the target. For each neighbour $k$ it holds its series $\mathbf{y}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 3}$, its availability mask, its formal standard errors, and its east and north offset from the target in kilometres. Missing days are set to zero and flagged; unused neighbour slots are padded and masked. A neighbour counts only if at least a quarter of its window is available, so that no slot is spent on a station that is not recording. The target's absolute coordinates are not part of $\mathcal{C}$, so that a model trained in California can be applied elsewhere. The task is to draw samples from $p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathcal{C})$.

\subsection{Training samples and network thinning}
\label{sec:thinning}

A training sample is a target station, a window lying entirely in the training years in which at least half of the target's days are available, and a network. Every admissible (station, window) pair is equally likely. The target's missing days are excluded from the loss.

Because the California network is far denser than the networks the model is meant for, each training network is thinned. In 80\% of samples the network is thinned to a median nearest-neighbour distance drawn log-uniformly from 10 to 100~km, and stations closer to the target than a distance drawn log-uniformly from 1 to 100~km are removed. The second rule prevents the model from relying on a nearby station and matches the intended use, a virtual station placed where there is none. In the remaining 20\% the full network is used with every neighbour, as in full-density evaluation: even the lightest thinning removes close pairs, whereas 13\% of the held-out stations have a training station within 5~km.

We thin by greedy minimum-separation thinning. Stations are visited in random order and kept if no kept station lies within a separation $s$, and $s$ is chosen by bisection to reach the required median spacing. Random subsampling, the obvious alternative, preserves the clustering of the California network and cannot produce a network resembling T\"urkiye's (Table~\ref{tab:thinning}): thinned at random to T\"urkiye's 60~km median spacing, California retains only 11--23 stations, with a long tail of distances beyond 100~km, whereas minimum-separation thinning retains 110--118 stations and matches T\"urkiye's median and upper tail. It has fewer close pairs than T\"urkiye (10th percentile 55 against 29~km); whether this matters will be tested on T\"urkiye rather than tuned for. Figure~\ref{fig:thinning} shows the networks used for evaluation. Thinnings are precomputed (64 spacings, four seeds each), since one thinning takes up to 0.1~s.

\subsection{Conditional diffusion model}
\label{sec:model}

The model follows the EDM formulation of \citet{karras2022edm}. The denoiser is $D(\mathbf{x}; \sigma) = c_\text{skip}(\sigma)\,\mathbf{x} + c_\text{out}(\sigma)\,F(c_\text{in}(\sigma)\,\mathbf{x}, \tfrac14 \ln\sigma; \mathcal{C})$ with the preconditioning of \citet{karras2022edm} and $\sigma_\text{data} = 1$, close to the standard deviation of the detrended, scaled targets (about 0.9). The network $F$ is a one-dimensional U-Net over time with 64, 128, 192 and 256 channels at resolutions $L$, $L/2$, $L/4$ and $L/8$, two residual blocks per level modulated by an embedding of the noise level, and the sine and cosine of the day of year as additional inputs. The neighbours are encoded once per sample, independently of the noise level: a convolution over each neighbour's series, availability and formal errors is followed by one residual block per U-Net resolution, shared by all neighbours and modulated by an embedding of the neighbour's offset and distance (offsets scaled by 100~km, the logarithm of the distance, and sines and cosines of the offsets at wavelengths of 10, 30, 100 and 300~km). After every residual block of the U-Net, each time step of the target attends to the $K$ neighbours at the same time step; a learned null token is always among the keys, so that padded neighbour slots and dropped conditioning are handled alike. Temporal self-attention operates at the two coarsest resolutions. The network has 11.1 million parameters.

Training draws $\ln\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(-1.2, 1.2^2)$ and minimises the weighted denoising error over the target days with data. The neighbours of 10\% of the samples are dropped, so that the model also learns the unconditional distribution and classifier-free guidance \citep{ho2022cfg} becomes available; it is not used here. We train for 100,000 steps of 64 samples with AdamW, a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-4}$ with 2,000 warm-up steps and cosine decay, gradient clipping at 1 and bfloat16 arithmetic, and sample from an exponential moving average of the weights (decay 0.999). Samples are drawn by integrating the probability-flow ordinary differential equation with Heun's method over 32 noise levels from 80 to 0.002 ($\rho = 7$); the procedure is deterministic given the initial noise, which is drawn from a seeded generator, as the paired comparisons of experiment B require. On a single RTX 3060 Ti, a training step takes 0.12~s and generating 32 samples for each of about 500 target windows takes 13.7~min.

\paragraph{The station's noise level} The model takes one optional input: the target station's own noise level, the logarithm of the robust scale of its short-period variations in the training years relative to that of a typical station, for each component, with a flag saying whether it is given. A small network maps the four numbers to a vector that is added to the noise-level embedding of every block; its last layer starts at zero. The level is hidden from 30\% of the training samples, so that one model generates with or without it. Section~\ref{sec:noise} shows why the input is needed. In use, it lets the user choose how noisy a virtual station should be; in evaluation, the held-out station's training-years level stands in for that choice, which uses information about the real station, so results without it are the main ones and results with it are reported separately.

\paragraph{Planted transients} California's training years contain few localised transients, and a model trained on them alone learns that what neighbours share is noise. With probability 0.8 a training sample is therefore drawn with one synthetic transient, added to the target and to every neighbour before the removal of each window's mean and trend. Its displacement field has a Gaussian footprint of radius 10--300~km (log-uniform), centred within 1.5 radii of the target, so that the target lies inside it, at its edge or outside it. In half of the transients the displacement has one direction everywhere (random azimuth, vertical share up to $\pm 0.5$); in the other half it is radial about the centre, with the horizontal displacement largest at one radius and the vertical at the centre, so that neighbours on opposite sides move in opposite directions. In time, 70\% are raised-cosine rises over 5--120~days with amplitudes of 1--30~mm (log-uniform), of which 30\% return to zero over one to three times the rise, and 30\% are steps with heavy-tailed amplitudes (Pareto, $\alpha = 1.5$, from 1~mm, capped at 50~mm); onsets lie between a quarter-window before the window and its end. A transient is kept only if at least two neighbours change by 1~mm or more over their available days. Otherwise the target could carry a transient that nothing in the input reveals, and the model would learn to add unexplained excursions. About 40\% of the drawn transients fail this test, so that 49\% of the training samples carry one. The family is deliberately simpler than the fault-slip events of experiment B, which therefore test generalisation rather than recall.

\paragraph{Training and comparisons} The validation loss falls from 0.66 after 2,000 steps to 0.58 at the end of training, still decreasing slowly as the learning rate decays (Figure~\ref{fig:training}). Two variants of the model serve as comparisons: the same model trained without planted transients, otherwise identical, and the same model retrained with a different random seed. The regression counterpart, \textbf{regress}, is the same network evaluated with a zero input and noise level, trained for 50,000 steps with the squared error on the target days with data, with the same planted transients and the same optional noise level, withheld in evaluation. It is the conditional mean that the diffusion model would reach if it predicted a single value; its validation error is lowest within 10,000 steps, while its training error keeps falling.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

Six baselines define what the diffusion model has to improve on: three simple ones, and three built on a covariance model of the residual field fitted to the training years.
\begin{description}
\item[Zero] predicts zero.
\item[IDW] predicts the weighted mean of the available neighbours on each day, with weights $w_k = \exp(-d_k^2 / 2\ell^2) / \bar\sigma_k^2$, where $d_k$ is the distance to neighbour $k$ and $\bar\sigma_k$ its median formal standard error in the window. The length scale $\ell$ is selected for each density among 10, 25, 50, 100, 200 and 500~km and $\infty$ by the short-period error on validation days, with 200 training stations as targets, each predicted without itself, so that the held-out stations play no part in the choice.
\item[IDW + noise] adds to the IDW prediction white and flicker noise \citep{williams2004}, independently per component, with periodogram level $P(f) = A + B/f$. For each window, $A$ and $B$ are fitted by non-negative least squares over 1/64 to 0.5 cycles per day to the departure of each neighbour from its own IDW prediction, and the medians over the neighbours are used. Fitting both terms jointly matters, since flicker noise retains power at high frequencies, and least squares is preferred to medians of periodogram ordinates, whose exponential distribution would bias the levels low by a factor $\ln 2$. The noise is synthesised on twice the window length and truncated, so that it does not wrap around. This is the simplest generative model of a virtual station.
\item[Kriging] predicts each day by Gaussian-process regression \citep{rasmussen2006} from the neighbours with data on that day. Each component has the stationary, isotropic covariance $C(d) = s_\mathrm{cm} + s_\mathrm{r} M(d/\ell) + s_\mathrm{n} \delta$, where $M$ is the Mat\'ern correlation with $\nu = 3/2$, $s_\mathrm{cm}$ a common mode shared by the whole network, $s_\mathrm{r}$ a regional part and $s_\mathrm{n}$ the station's own variance; the common-mode term acts as the unknown mean of ordinary kriging with a fitted prior. The covariance is fitted once, to the training years of the training stations, and serves every density: the mean covariance of all station pairs within 300~km in 14 distance classes and the mean station variance, over 256-day windows detrended as the model's inputs, are fitted by weighted least squares, with $\ell$ by grid search. Station-windows with a listed earthquake step larger than one robust scale are left out. Nearly all the shared covariance is network-wide: it is constant from 25 to 300~km, and the regional part is small and confined to the first 20~km ($s_\mathrm{cm}$ = 0.26, 0.14 and 0.18 of station variances of 0.65, 0.41 and 0.80 for east, north and up, in scaled units).
\item[Kriging + noise] adds noise to the kriging prediction as IDW + noise does, fitted to the departures of the neighbours from their own kriging predictions.
\item[Space--time Gaussian process] generates the target's whole window jointly. Each component is the sum of three independent parts, each separable in space and time: the common mode, the regional part (Mat\'ern, $\ell$ from the kriging fit) and the station's own part, scaled by the square of the station's noise level relative to a typical station. The temporal covariance of each part is the sum of white noise, flicker noise and a first-order Gauss--Markov process \citep{williams2004,langbein2008}; without the Gauss--Markov term the station part falls short of the observed covariance at lags of 6--16 days. The parts are fitted to lag curves of the training years between 0 and 128 days, computed exactly for a detrended window: from pairs 100--300~km apart (common mode), pairs within 10~km (regional) and each station with itself (station part), with amplitudes by non-negative least squares and the Gauss--Markov time constant by grid search. Space--time interaction \citep{gneiting2002} would act only on the small regional part and is omitted. Samples are drawn from the target's distribution conditional on every available neighbour value in the window, with a flat prior on each neighbour's mean and trend \citep[eq.~2.42]{rasmussen2006}, which conditions on the neighbours as detrended. Covariances in the lag curves are medians over stations or station pairs within each window, averaged over windows. The residuals are heavy-tailed: in the training years the mean variance of a 256-day window is 1.5--1.7 times the median, so a Gaussian model fitted to means describes a disturbed station rather than a typical one; fitted to means, its samples were 25\% too large at short periods and its CRPS worse than that of IDW + noise.
\end{description}
Related methods that are not reimplemented here are reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:related}.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experiment A: held-out stations}

Each of the 173 held-out stations is generated from the training stations, thinned to one of four densities: the full network, and median spacings of 25, 60 and 100~km. The 60~km density matches T\"urkiye and is the main result. Each thinned density is repeated with five thinning seeds. The validation period is tiled with 256-day windows; the last window is aligned to the end of the period and scores only the days not covered by an earlier window. All results are on the validation period; the test period (2022 on) is reserved for a single final evaluation.

Errors are expressed in units of each station's robust scale over the training years, every score is computed per station, and stations are summarised by the median, following the preceding benchmark. Days within 30 days of an earthquake of magnitude 6 or more that NGL lists as possibly offsetting the station are excluded from the short- and long-period scores, and held-out stations whose scatter increased more than threefold, or whose series jumped without a listed cause, after 2019 are excluded from scoring (one of the 173; the rule is applied to the whole cube and drops 19 of its 1,251 stations).

\begin{description}
\item[Point accuracy.] The error of the sample mean is split into a long-period part, its 61-day running median, and a short-period part, the remainder; the normalised root-mean-square error (nRMSE) of each, and of the total, is reported.
\item[Probabilistic accuracy.] The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) and its multivariate counterpart, the energy score \citep{gneiting2007}, are computed with the fair ensemble estimators of \citet{ferro2014}, which are unbiased for the distribution the samples are drawn from. For a point forecast the CRPS reduces to the absolute error. Coverage of the central 50\% and 90\% intervals is computed with Weibull plotting positions \citep{hyndman1996}, under which a draw from the ensemble's own distribution falls inside the 90\% interval with probability 0.9; the default positions of common software give about 0.85 for 32 members. The ratio of ensemble spread to the error of the ensemble mean is 1 for a calibrated ensemble. For the short-period scores the truth and each sample are decomposed separately. Because a median over stations emphasises the typical station, calibration is also reported pooled over all scored days of all stations, and separately for the quartiles of the held-out stations' own noise level, the robust scale of their short-period variations in the training years relative to that of a typical station. The observed series were screened for outliers when the data cube was built (Section~\ref{sec:data}); for a like-for-like comparison, generative methods are also scored after the same screen is applied to every sample, with a flagged value replaced by the sample's running median.
\item[Realism.] Single samples are compared with the truth in four ways: the ratio of their power spectral densities in six period bands from 2 to 128 days, the ratio of the robust scales of their short-period parts, the ratio of the 99th percentiles of their absolute short-period values, and their correlation with the neighbours as a function of distance.
\item[Indistinguishability.] A classifier two-sample test \citep{lopezpaz2017} asks whether generated windows can be told from real ones. The real and one generated version of each 256-day window of the held-out stations in the validation years, both detrended and scaled, the generated one with the real one's gaps and screened for outliers as the observations were, are split by station into two folds; a classifier is trained on one fold and tested on the other, and the folds are swapped. The primary classifier is a logistic regression on summary features of each window: the log scales of its fast and slow parts, the ratio of its 99th percentile to its scale, its autocorrelation at lags of 1 and 7 days, its log power in six period bands and, in the conditional variant, its correlation and scale ratio with the mean of its four nearest neighbours. A small convolutional network on the series is a second check, and IDW without noise is a positive control. An accuracy of 0.5 means the two are indistinguishable; an exact binomial test gives its significance, and a paired test and a bootstrap over stations agree with it.
\end{description}
Differences between two methods are tested on the same stations and networks. The difference of the median summaries has a 95\% interval from a bootstrap over stations (10,000 resamples), in which a station keeps all its thinned networks together, and the number of stations at which each method is better is tested with an exact sign test. Both treat stations as independent; nearby stations share signal, so the intervals are somewhat optimistic, while the counts of stations are robust to it.

\subsection{Experiment B: planted fault-slip events}

To test whether localised transients are transferred to a virtual station, slow-slip and afterslip events are placed on fault surfaces of the SCEC Community Fault Model \citep{plesch2007,plesch2024cfm}, and their surface displacements are computed with triangular dislocations in an elastic half-space \citep{nikkhoo2015}, with Poisson's ratio 0.25. Two templates follow published events: shallow slow slip on steep strike-slip faults, 8--25~km long, from the surface to 2--4~km depth, with 0.8--4.5~cm of slip propagating along strike at 0.4--9~km per day \citep{materna2024,vavra2024}; and afterslip on 15--50~km patches between the surface and 5--12~km depth, with 10--40~cm of slip growing as $\log(1 + t/\tau)$ over a year, $\tau$ = 17--34~days \citep{gonzalezortega2014}. Sixty events of each template are placed on random suitable faults, with onsets in the validation period such that each event and a further 60 days end within it, and each is kept only if at least one scored held-out station moves by more than 1~mm horizontally. The radius of the region in which an event's final horizontal displacement exceeds 1~mm is its footprint: 5--17~km (median 11~km) for slow slip and 26--103~km (median 54~km) for afterslip. Fault surfaces are taken as triangulated in the fault model, except that vertices less than 0.5~m apart horizontally are given one horizontal position: the conversion of the model's coordinates leaves its vertical edges about a millimetre off vertical, and for such nearly vertical triangle sides the half-space solution is ill-conditioned and gives a displacement that does not decay with distance (one afterslip event would otherwise move three quarters of the network by more than 1~mm).

Each event is added to every station's real series, the held-out targets included, and every held-out target that it moves by more than 1~mm is generated twice over the windows of experiment A that overlap the event, once from the network with the event and once from the original network. Both runs use the same random numbers for each target and window, whatever else is generated with them, so that the difference $\mathbf{d}$ of the two sample means (16 samples) isolates what the method passed on from the event. Detrending is linear, so the planted signal in the detrended truth is the detrended event displacement $\mathbf{s}$ at the target. The recovered fraction is $\rho = \langle\mathbf{d}, \mathbf{s}\rangle / \langle\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{s}\rangle$ over the horizontal components and the event's days (from the onset to 60~days after its end), reported per target and event and pooled over them weighted by $\langle\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{s}\rangle$. The shape (correlation of $\mathbf{d}$ with $\mathbf{s}$), the CRPS and coverage of the planted truth by the planted samples, and the spurious signal at up to five held-out targets that the event leaves still but whose neighbours it moves are also reported. Per-window detrending removes most of a signal that grows over months: an afterslip event that moves a station by 12~mm leaves a detrended signal of 0.4~mm r.m.s., so $\rho$ measures the transfer of what survives detrending, as for the data. Experiment B uses the full network and two thinnings at each of 25, 60 and 100~km.

As a real event, held-out stations within 300~km of the 2019 Ridgecrest earthquakes are generated over a 256-day window centred on them, and the coseismic offset of the samples (mean of 10 days after minus 10 days before, 2 days either side skipped) is compared with the offset observed at the station. The window lies in the training years, but the held-out stations never entered training, and the stations near the rupture were excluded from it in the second half of 2019.

\subsection{Experiment C: T\"urkiye}

Every station is generated in turn from all the others, its 16 nearest within 300~km, at the network's own density. Nothing is tuned on T\"urkiye: the trained models and their input scaling, the length scale of IDW (California's at 60~km, T\"urkiye's spacing) and the covariances of kriging and the space--time Gaussian process all come from California, and a station's noise level is relative to California's typical station, as the models learned it. Scoring follows experiment A with three changes: the quality control that drops stations for site trouble checks the scored period's own days, so that a station is not dropped from 2013--2015 for trouble years later; stations with too short a record before the fit's end, whose trajectory is fitted to the start of their record and so overlaps the scored days, are left out; and the Kahramanmara\c{s} window is skipped. The Kahramanmara\c{s} sequence is analysed separately: the coseismic offsets and the first year of postseismic deformation of virtual stations near the rupture are compared with the observed ones, as a function of distance from the rupture and from the nearest station.

\paragraph{Point accuracy depends little on density} Predicting a held-out station from its neighbours removes much of its short-period error: IDW reduces the fast nRMSE from 0.776 for predicting zero to 0.560 with the full network. The reduction hardly depends on density: at T\"urkiye's density of 60~km the fast nRMSE is 0.565, and even at 100~km it is 0.586. The length scales selected on validation are long (200~km to infinite), so all neighbours are weighted nearly equally. Both observations indicate that the short-period variability a station shares with its neighbours is dominated by common-mode error coherent over hundreds of kilometres \citep{wdowinski1997,dong2006}, which a sparse network samples almost as well as a dense one. Regress, the most accurate point predictor, improves on IDW by only 0.5--1.4\%, and kriging matches IDW at every density: with a shared signal that is almost constant over 300~km, the best linear weights are nearly uniform, and the slight shrinkage of kriging towards zero costs up to 0.003. The short-period point scores are therefore only weakly sensitive to network density; what densification contributes beyond the common mode has to be measured on spatially localised signals (Section~\ref{sec:resultsb}). After each window's mean and trend are removed, the slow part contributes little error (nRMSE 0.13--0.14 for every method but zero, 0.28 for zero).

\paragraph{Noise makes the averages calibrated generators} Adding white and flicker noise to the IDW mean lowers the fast CRPS from 0.421 to 0.308 at full density and from 0.423 to 0.310 at 60~km, a reduction of 27\%. The mean of its 32 samples is slightly less accurate than IDW itself (fast nRMSE 0.567 against 0.560), since the mean of a finite ensemble retains part of the added noise. The samples are close to calibrated at the median station: the 90\% intervals cover 91\% of the fast part and the 50\% intervals 55--56\%, and the ratio of spread to error of the total is 0.94--0.95. Kriging + noise behaves alike (fast CRPS 0.309--0.321).

\paragraph{The diffusion model is the most accurate generator} The diffusion model has the lowest short-period CRPS at every density: 0.297, 0.299, 0.302 and 0.309 for the full network and for 25, 60 and 100~km, against 0.312, 0.310, 0.312 and 0.319 for the space--time Gaussian process and 0.308--0.321 for IDW + noise. Paired on the same stations and networks, it is 3.3--4.9\% better than the Gaussian process, 2.7--3.8\% better than IDW + noise and 3.3--4.3\% better than kriging + noise, every 95\% interval excludes zero, and it is better at 99--105 of the 107 stations (Table~\ref{tab:significance}). Its energy score over the three components is also the lowest (0.624 at 60~km, against 0.643 and 0.645 for IDW + noise and the Gaussian process). The mean of its samples is as accurate a point prediction as IDW (fast nRMSE 0.564 and 0.569 with the full network and at 60~km). The lead is not an accident of one training run: the model retrained with a different seed scores within 0.2\% of it at full density and 60~km, with intervals that include zero, and keeps the same lead over the Gaussian process ($-4.9$ and $-3.1\%$).

\paragraph{The spatial structure} The correlation of a real station's short-period variations with those of its neighbours decreases with distance, from 0.51 within 10~km to 0.45--0.47 beyond (Figure~\ref{fig:corr}). The samples of IDW + noise correlate with their neighbours at 0.49--0.50 beyond 10~km, more strongly than real stations do, and without the decrease: a near-uniform weighted mean with independent noise reproduces the average amount of shared signal but not how it varies in space. Kriging + noise, whose small regional covariance gives near neighbours more weight, the Gaussian process and the diffusion model reproduce the decrease: the mean absolute error of their neighbour correlation over distance classes is 0.007--0.017 across densities, against 0.033--0.052 for IDW + noise. Figures~\ref{fig:example} and~\ref{fig:example_diffusion} show one held-out station with samples of IDW + noise and of the diffusion model.

\paragraph{Planted transients cost nothing on ordinary days} The model trained without planted transients, otherwise identical, scores the same on held-out stations: paired differences in fast CRPS between $-0.0$ and $+0.2\%$ at every density, with 95\% intervals that include zero (Tables~\ref{tab:significance} and~\ref{tab:transients}), and validation losses of 0.581 and 0.582. Without the noise level the model trained with transients is slightly wider and heavier-tailed (ratio of spread to error 1.19--1.20 against 1.16, tail 0.17--0.18 against 0.16), as if it allowed for a transient that might be present. Regress is likewise unchanged by the transients (within 0.3\%).

\paragraph{Only events larger than the gaps between stations reach a virtual station} No method passes on a planted event whose footprint is smaller than about twice the distance from the target to its nearest network station (Figure~\ref{fig:brho}): if no neighbour moves, nothing can be transferred. Shallow slow slip, with footprints of about 11~km, is therefore invisible at every density (pooled $\rho \le 0.03$; Table~\ref{tab:brho}), and so is afterslip once the network is thinned to 60~km or more ($\rho \le 0.01$). The transfer of localised signals is where densification matters, in contrast to the common-mode noise of experiment A.

\paragraph{The diffusion model and the Gaussian process transfer the most} On the full network, the diffusion model recovers a median of 41\% of the planted signal per target and event, the space--time Gaussian process 37\%, and IDW, IDW + noise and kriging + noise 12--23\%. Where the footprint is more than four times the distance to the nearest station, the Gaussian process recovers the most on the full network (95\% against 88\% for the diffusion model and 76--83\% for the averages); over all networks the diffusion model recovers more than the Gaussian process both there (62\% against 57\%) and where the footprint is two to four times the gap (22\% against 10\%), and on the networks thinned to 25~km its median is 0.15--0.19 against 0.08--0.13. These medians are over target--event pairs, so events seen by several targets, which are the larger ones, count more; taking each event's median first lowers every value (full network: Gaussian process 0.18, diffusion 0.10, IDW 0.03). Pooled by signal energy over afterslip on the full network the Gaussian process recovers the most (0.25, against 0.22 for the diffusion model and 0.17--0.19 for the averages), and the shape of what it passes on is the closest to the event (median correlation 0.97 against 0.92); conditioning on the whole window, with a regional component correlated over months, suits a slow, regionally coherent signal. The pooled fraction is dominated by a few targets within 25~km of an event: five of the 114 afterslip target--event pairs carry 45\% of the detrended signal energy, and at four of these every method recovers less than half, the diffusion model with the wrong sign at two of them. During the events the diffusion model keeps its probabilistic lead (CRPS of the planted truth 0.316 against 0.321 for the Gaussian process, coverage of 90\% intervals 0.83 against 0.81), and it adds the least spurious signal at stations outside an event whose neighbours it moves (median 2.8\% of the neighbours' peak displacement on the full network, against 3.2--4.5\% for the others).

\paragraph{The transfer comes from the training data} Trained without planted transients, the same model passes on much less: a median of 23\% on the full network, 13\% where the footprint is two to four times the gap (29\% with transients) and 39\% over all networks where it is more than four times the gap; on the full network, where the footprint is more than four times the gap, it passes on less than the near-uniform averages (75\% against 83\%). Given the station's noise level, the model transfers as much as without it (median 0.41). Regress gains as much from the planted transients as the diffusion model does (median 0.44 against 0.21 on the full network, and the largest energy-pooled fraction of afterslip, 0.37), so the transfer comes from the training data rather than from the generative formulation; what the generative formulation adds is the probabilistic accuracy and the calibrated range of Section~\ref{sec:resultsa}.

\paragraph{A real earthquake} Of 35 held-out stations within 300~km of the Ridgecrest earthquakes with data around them, 12 moved by more than 5~mm (Table~\ref{tab:ridgecrest}). With the full network the averaging methods reproduce their offsets (recovered fraction 0.98--1.03, median error 2.7--3.6~mm), and the diffusion model recovers 0.85 of them with the smallest error (2.2~mm); 79\% of the offset components lie inside its 90\% range, against 17--21\% for the Gaussian generators. With the network thinned to 60~km, every method trained without planted transients recovers little (0.10--0.22, errors 11.1--12.7~mm), whereas the diffusion model recovers 0.61--0.66 (without and with the noise level) with a median error of 3.7~mm; its error is smaller than that of every method trained without transients at 11 of the 12 stations, and its generated offsets point in the observed directions. Regress recovers more of the offsets (0.83) but with a larger error (5.7~mm). Only TOWG, 15~km from the rupture with an offset of 0.55~m and its nearest network station 35~km away, is missed by every method. The Gaussian process fails at 60~km because its regional covariance, fitted to ordinary days, decays within 5--21~km: with the nearest stations 14--41~km from the targets, it attributes most of a step shared by the neighbours to their own noise. The models trained with transients have learned that displacement can be coherent over tens to hundreds of kilometres, which the far field of a magnitude 7 earthquake is. The planted family contains such steps by design (up to 50~mm, footprints up to 300~km), but it was drawn without reference to Ridgecrest, whose days and near-field stations were excluded from training.

\paragraph{The models transfer without retraining} In the main period (2013--2015), when the national network records densely, the diffusion model has the lowest short-period CRPS and energy score of all methods (Table~\ref{tab:cscores}): 0.369 without the station's noise level and 0.368 with it, against 0.376 and 0.375 for the space--time Gaussian process and 0.380 for IDW + noise and kriging + noise. The paired differences to the Gaussian process are $-1.8\%$ in both cases, with 95\% intervals that exclude zero, and the diffusion model is better at 135--138 of the 150 compared stations (Table~\ref{tab:csignificance}); over the stations in T\"urkiye alone the difference is $-2.0\%$ (125 of 135 stations). No part of any method was fitted to T\"urkiye. The margin is smaller than in California ($-3.3$ to $-4.9\%$; Table~\ref{tab:significance}) but as consistent across stations.

\paragraph{Point accuracy and calibration} As in California, the methods hardly differ in point accuracy: the fast nRMSE of the main period is 0.688 for IDW, 0.689 for regress and 0.691 for the Gaussian process and the mean of the diffusion samples. They differ in calibration. The Gaussian process, close to calibrated at the median Californian station (ratio of spread to error 1.03), is too narrow in T\"urkiye (0.84, coverage of 90\% fast intervals 0.88). The diffusion model is slightly too wide (1.12, coverage 0.90), as it is in California without the station's noise level, and given the level it comes closer to calibrated (1.05). Giving the noise level changes the CRPS of either method by less than 1\% in every period.

\paragraph{The sparse network} From 2016 on, with about 17 stations recording on a typical day, the two model-based generators pull well ahead of the averages: the diffusion model is 8.1\% better than IDW + noise (78 of 86 stations), and the Gaussian process 7\% better. Between them the difference is small. The diffusion model is better at 64 of 86 stations, which a sign test finds significant ($p < 10^{-5}$), but the difference of the median scores ($-1.1\%$, interval $-2.7$ to $+1.2\%$) does not exclude zero; on the sparse network the two are on par. The diffusion samples are also heavier-tailed here than the truth: the 99th percentile of their absolute short-period values exceeds the truth's by 31\% ($\log_2$ ratio 0.39), against 6\% in the main period.

\paragraph{Distance to the nearest station} The diffusion model has the lowest median CRPS in every class of distance to the nearest recording station and in every period, including targets 75~km or more from any station (Table~\ref{tab:cdistance}). Its advantage over the Gaussian process ranges from 0.5 to 3.8\% without a systematic trend with distance; classes of 5 to 27 stations are too small to resolve one.

\paragraph{Scoring inside the fit years} With the trajectories fitted to 2019, as in California, and the years 2009--2015 scored inside the fit, every method's CRPS is lower (diffusion 0.349, the Gaussian process 0.355, IDW + noise 0.359), but the comparison is unchanged: the diffusion model is better than the Gaussian process by 1.5\% without the noise level and 1.9\% with it, at 154--159 of 164 stations. Scoring inside the fit flatters all methods alike and does not change their ranking.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What a generative model adds} On held-out stations the diffusion model improves on the best Gaussian generator by a few per cent in CRPS, consistently across stations, densities, training seeds and regions. The gain is not in point accuracy, which is bounded by a common mode that any near-uniform average captures, but in the shape of the distribution: only the diffusion samples pass a classifier test as real stations, whereas every Gaussian generator, including a space--time Gaussian process fitted to the same training years, is recognised about 80\% of the time. Reproducing the decrease of inter-station correlation with distance, by contrast, does not require a learned generator; kriging + noise and the Gaussian process reproduce it as well.

\paragraph{The station's own noise} The neighbours do not reveal how noisy the station at a location would be, and every generator that is not told hedges over it, too wide for quiet stations and too narrow for noisy ones. This is a property of the task rather than of a model, and it makes the median station a misleading summary of calibration. Given the level, the diffusion model is calibrated across stations of every noise level; the Gaussian process is not, because the level scales its width but not its tails. For a virtual station the level is a choice of the user, a statement about the kind of monument assumed.

\paragraph{Localised signals} Densification matters for signals with a spatial footprint comparable to the station spacing. A virtual station can recover such a signal only if neighbours see it, which for the planted events requires a footprint at least about twice the distance to the nearest station; shallow slow slip, with footprints of about 10~km, is invisible at every density, and afterslip at spacings of 60~km and more. Where events are large enough, how much is passed on depends on the training data more than on the formulation: trained on ordinary years alone, the diffusion model passes on no more than a weighted mean, and trained with simple synthetic transients, it and its regression counterpart pass on as much as or more than a Gaussian process, and reproduce the offsets of a real earthquake from a network as sparse as T\"urkiye's. The Gaussian process remains the best at the shape of slow, regionally coherent signals.

\paragraph{Limitations} The model is trained in one region; T\"urkiye shows that it transfers, but with a smaller margin and with heavier tails than the truth on the sparse network of later years. Without the noise level, a few samples drift over months without cause (Figure~\ref{fig:example_diffusion}), and the classifier detects the samples weakly at spacings of 60~km and more. The planted-event results are medians over target--event pairs, which favour large events; per event every method recovers less. The largest afterslip signals, at stations close to an event, are passed on only in part by every method. Windows are generated independently and not yet stitched into long series. Stations are treated as independent in the paired tests, which makes the bootstrap intervals somewhat optimistic; the counts of stations are not affected. All results are on the validation period; the test period has not been used.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

A conditional diffusion model generates daily GNSS residual series at locations without a station from the stations around it. Trained in California with networks thinned to the spacing of sparse networks and with synthetic transients planted in its inputs, it is the most accurate probabilistic generator of held-out stations at every density, by 3--5\% in CRPS over a space--time Gaussian process, its samples are the only ones a classifier cannot tell from real stations, it is calibrated across stations once told the station's noise level, and it passes on localised signals larger than the gaps between stations, including the offsets of the 2019 Ridgecrest earthquakes from a network thinned to 60~km. Applied without retraining to T\"urkiye it remains the best method. The remaining work is the Kahramanmara\c{s} case study, which needs a published rupture geometry, the stitching of windows into long series, and the single evaluation on the test period.
